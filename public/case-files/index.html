Hospitals

Hand hygiene in hospital wards

Improve compliance without a training module.

Problem
Hand-hygiene compliance sits below target and annual training does not hold it.
Approach
Pause the training module on one ward. Install a pressure tile at the door that lights a path to the sanitiser dispenser. No classroom time.
Requirements
8 weeks, one ward plus a control ward, tile and dispenser retrofit, one observer.
Target
Entry hand rub from the audited baseline to 75 percent by week 8 (target). Infection rate read at six months.
Needed from you
Four quarters of audit data, one infection-control contact, permission to pause the module on one ward.

Mechanic

Step on the tile, the path lights up, use the dispenser, the light goes off. Twelve correct entries in a row raise a paper flag at the nurse station. A missed entry does nothing; there is no penalty.

Learning objective (target)

By week 8 of the pilot, ward staff perform hand rub at ward entry on at least 75 percent of covertly observed entries, up from the ward's audited baseline, with no verbal prompting.

Evaluation, Kirkpatrick levels

  • 1 Reaction. Two-question card at shift end, dropped in a box.
  • 2 Learning. Cue-recognition test (photo of a doorway: where is the moment?), not a knowledge quiz.
  • 3 Behaviour. Covert observation and dispenser consumption per patient-day, before and after, against a control ward.
  • 4 Results. Healthcare-associated infection rate over six months, owned by infection control.

Ethics

No individual tracking. No sound loud enough to shame. Patient rooms stay out of scope.

  • Fogg, Behaviour Model. Put the prompt where ability is highest: hand free, body already moving through a door.
  • Gollwitzer, implementation intentions. "When I cross the tile, I rub" binds cue to act without a decision.
  • Van Gennep, Turner. Rites of passage, liminality. Thresholds already carry ritual weight in every culture (the genkan). The design borrows it.
  • Graybiel, basal ganglia and habit chunking. Fixed cue, fixed routine, immediate sensory reward. Nothing variable.
  • Schultz, reward prediction error. For hygiene you want reliability, so feedback is constant and immediate. Variable reward belongs in slot machines, not wards.
  • Deci and Ryan, self-determination theory. A team flag protects autonomy and relatedness. Surveillance destroys both.

Concept study, 2026. Targets only, no results.

Warehouses and logistics

Warehouse onboarding

Replace the 2-hour induction with 12 short exercises on the floor.

Problem
A two-hour slide induction; errors and near-misses cluster in week one.
Approach
Cut the induction to 20 minutes. New pickers do 12 exercises, each under 7 minutes, on live shelves with a buddy who signs them off.
Requirements
One cohort of 10 to 20, two rostered buddies, a card deck, coloured tape.
Target
Day-7 pick accuracy to the 90-day average; week-one near-misses to the site median by day 30 (targets).
Needed from you
WMS access for four metrics, two buddies on the roster, the safety session kept to 20 minutes.

Mechanic

A deck of 12 cards, one skill per card: scan, reach, lift, confirm, pack, handle exceptions. Three stages per card: do it with the buddy watching, do it alone, then teach it to the next new hire.

Learning objectives (targets)

By day 7, new pickers complete a 40-line order unassisted with pick accuracy at or above the site's 90-day-tenure average. By day 30, near-miss reports per 1,000 picks for new starters match the site median.

Evaluation, Kirkpatrick levels

  • 1 Reaction. One tick box on each card: useful, or not.
  • 2 Learning. Exercise pass rates, first attempt against retry, from the cards.
  • 3 Behaviour. Pick accuracy and near-miss rate at day 7 and day 30, pulled from the warehouse management system.
  • 4 Results. Time to full productivity and week-one incident rate, this cohort against the last two.

Ethics

Exercise records belong to the picker. Supervisors see pass or fail, never the number of attempts.

  • Ericsson, deliberate practice. Short, targeted, immediate feedback. Nothing a slide can do.
  • Bjork, desirable difficulties. Spacing and retrieval beat a massed two-hour session on retention.
  • Roediger and Karpicke, the testing effect. Every kata is a retrieval, not a review.
  • Sweller, cognitive load. One micro-skill per card keeps intrinsic load inside working memory.
  • Csikszentmihalyi, flow. Tape colours tune challenge to skill as the week goes on.
  • Lave and Wenger, legitimate peripheral participation. Teaching the next hire makes the learner a member of the team, not a trainee.
  • Shu-Ha-Ri. A Japanese three-stage training progression: learn, adapt, master.

Concept study, 2026. Based on buddy onboarding run for junior designers at three studios.

Schools, Grade 5, India

Fractions for Grade 5

Teach fractions with a sharing game before introducing numbers.

Problem
Grade 5 fraction misconceptions survive years of worksheets.
Approach
Four sessions of a table game using paper rotis. Written fractions are introduced in session three, after students have compared pieces by hand.
Requirements
Four periods, two classes, a control class, paper and scissors, 30 minutes with each teacher.
Target
80 percent order unit fractions and name an equivalent pair, two weeks after (target).
Needed from you
Two classes, one control class, the ten-item diagnostic run before and after.

Mechanic

Four players, one paper roti per round, a card says how many people are sharing. Fold, cut, compare. The game moves on only when the table agrees who got more. Equivalent fractions appear naturally when two players cut differently and get the same amount.

Learning objective (target)

After four sessions, at least 80 percent of students correctly order unit fractions with denominators 2 to 8 and name one equivalent pair, on a diagnostic that uses no roti imagery.

Evaluation, Kirkpatrick levels

  • 1 Reaction. One drawing per child after each session.
  • 2 Learning. Pre and post diagnostic, same items, four weeks apart.
  • 3 Behaviour. Transfer to number-line tasks the class has never seen.
  • 4 Results. Teacher interview: what changed in ordinary lessons.

Ethics

No screens. Rotis respect what the class already eats; swap in the local flatbread elsewhere.

  • Bruner, enactive to iconic to symbolic. Cut, then draw, then write. In that order and no faster.
  • Papert, constructionism. Knowledge built through a thing the child made.
  • Montessori, manipulatives. The object carries the concept until the symbol can.
  • Vygotsky, zone of proximal development. The argument at the table is the scaffold; the teacher only referees.
  • Lakoff and Núñez, embodied mathematics. Fractions as portions of a whole body knows, before symbols.
  • Huizinga, Homo Ludens. The rule (fair share) precedes the concept. Play comes before culture.

Concept study, 2026. Targets only, no results.

Physiotherapy clinics

Physiotherapy adherence

A physical bead counter instead of an app with streaks.

Problem
Fewer than half of patients finish home physio. Apps add streaks, streaks add shame.
Approach
A loop of 10 beads and a paper tally sheet. No app. The physiotherapist checks both at each visit.
Requirements
One clinic, two physiotherapists, 25 loops, six weeks.
Target
70 percent of prescribed sessions completed against usual care (target). Function score at six weeks.
Needed from you
Two physios, the current adherence sheet, service-evaluation sign-off.

Mechanic

The physiotherapist sets the number of sets in clinic. One rep, one bead; the set is done when the marker returns to the knot. A paper tally sheet gets one tick per session. Optional second loop for a family member who exercises alongside.

Learning objective (target)

Over six weeks, patients using the bead counter report completing at least 70 percent of prescribed sessions, against usual care, with no worsening in pain scores.

Evaluation, Kirkpatrick levels

  • 1 Reaction. One question at each visit: did the loop get in the way?
  • 2 Learning. Patient demonstrates the set unprompted at visit two.
  • 3 Behaviour. Scroll ticks and bead state, corroborated at visits.
  • 4 Results. Patient-Specific Functional Scale at six weeks, against usual care.

Ethics

No data leaves the room by default. The object is not connected. A missed day changes nothing about the object; pain is respected.

  • Deci and Ryan, self-determination theory. Autonomy (patient sets the pace), competence (the marker comes home), relatedness (the physio reads the beads; the family loop).
  • Vlaeyen and Linton, fear-avoidance model. Graded exposure, no punishment for a missed day, so avoidance never gets a reason.
  • Winnicott, transitional objects. A thing that holds the relationship between visits.
  • Mala practice, Indian traditions. Counting as a calming act, 108 beads. This counter uses ten.
  • Fogg, anchoring. The loop lives where the exercise happens, so the cue is the place.
  • Schultz, reward prediction error. Small, predictable completion beats a surprise badge for someone managing pain.

Concept study, 2026. Closest to current work on therapeutic game design.

05

Banking apps

First-salary savings simulation

Get first-time earners to set up automatic savings.

Problem
First-jobbers read about compounding and change nothing in the bank.
Approach
A 6-minute choice-based simulation inside the app onboarding. At the end, users see the outcome of the choices they did not make, and one button to set up an automatic transfer.
Requirements
Five weeks to a live A/B, 2,000 users per arm.
Target
25 percent set up a payday auto-transfer within 90 days, against the article cohort (target).
Needed from you
Funnel access, the verified transfer event, a no-placement line in the contract.

Mechanic

Twelve turns: rent, a family transfer, a trip with friends, a phone EMI, an emergency. Choices are never labelled good or bad. At month 36 the user sees their balance next to the two paths they did not take. One button sets up a payday auto-transfer.

Learning objective (target)

Within 90 days, at least 25 percent of players who reach the reveal have set up an automatic payday transfer, verified by the partner, against a control group who read the same content as an article.

Evaluation, Kirkpatrick levels

  • 1 Reaction. One tap at the reveal.
  • 2 Learning. Two-question mental-model check: does starting earlier or investing more matter more?
  • 3 Behaviour. The 90-day auto-transfer rate.
  • 4 Results. Partner's 12-month balance retention for the cohort.

Ethics

No product placement inside the turns. The auto-transfer button names the partner. Scenarios reflect Indian first-job reality, including family transfers, without judging them.

  • Kahneman and Tversky, prospect theory. The reveal frames the counterfactual as a loss, which the mind weighs about twice as heavily.
  • Laibson, present bias. Turns compress time so the future stops being discounted.
  • Thaler, mental accounting. The auto-transfer creates the account before the temptation does.
  • Kolb, experiential learning. Concrete experience before abstract concept, every time.
  • Bateson, the play frame. The game frame makes regret safe enough to learn from.
  • Sociology of remittance, Indian family norms. The family transfer is a choice with dignity inside the game, not a mistake to be corrected.

Concept study, 2026. Choice-based stories are the format shipped most often.

How learning is designed

Six steps. Every project plan on this page follows them. Click a step for the question it answers and the research behind it.

1. First contact

What does the learner see or touch first, before any explanation?

People engage faster when the first thing they meet is something to do, not something to read (Bateson on the play frame; Winnicott on safe objects). In the hand-hygiene plan, the first thing a nurse meets is a floor tile, not a poster.

08

How results are measured

Every project starts with a written objective and a measurement plan.

The objective

By day 7, new pickers complete a 40-line order unassisted at or above the site's 90-day average accuracy.

Every objective names who, what they will do, under what conditions, and to what standard.

The measurement plan

ReactionOne question, asked immediately.
LearningA demonstration, not a quiz.
BehaviourReal data from the business: audits, system logs, records.
ResultsBusiness results, measured against a comparison group.

Not built, on principle

  • Individual leaderboards in health or safety settings.
  • Streaks that punish a missed day.
  • Variable rewards on anything a person must do while hurt, tired or afraid.
  • Dark patterns. Anything designed to work on people without their knowledge.
